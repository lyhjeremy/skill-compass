SkillCompass
know where you stand

Every skill quiz hands you a percentile.

This one hides it until the number is real.

SkillCompass is a two-minute skill check across data, software, cloud, security, finance, marketing, product and more. Six questions, chosen to match your level, each one written against a named public source. At the end you get a map of what you actually know. What you don't get is a confident-sounding statistic invented out of a sample of one.

The questions recalibrate from real answers

Each item ships with an estimated difficulty and then moves toward what people actually score on it, using the same Elo update chess ratings use. Your six questions are picked to match the level one placement question suggests.

Every question names its source

The explanation under each answer cites the public reference it was written against, so a disputed key is checkable rather than a matter of trusting the quiz.

The scoring can't go down

Questions ship as static JSON and the whole quiz loop runs in your browser. No server call sits between you and your result. The AI extras are the only part that needs a backend.

No account, and the data is yours

No sign-up, no cookies past localStorage. Your history lives on your device and there are export and erase buttons for it. The only central record is an anonymous answer log.

Bearing · the problem

You can't grade your own SQL

Ask someone to rate their own SQL out of ten and you learn how they feel, not what they know. Job descriptions have the same problem in reverse: a posting asks for "strong SQL" without saying whether that means a join or a window function. Between those two vague numbers sits the actual question, which is what would you get wrong.

A short test can answer that, but only if the test is honest about its own accuracy. Most aren't. They ask six easy questions, congratulate you, and quote a percentile computed from nothing.

↓ the check

Six questions, about two minutes, untimed

One placement question comes first and sets the difficulty band for the rest. Answer it well and the following six come from the hard set; miss it and they come from an easier one, so the check spends its questions where they tell it something. You can also skip the placement and pick the band yourself.

Nothing is timed. Your pace is recorded and shown back to you afterward as seconds per question, because reading speed is information, but it never costs you points.

One quick question first sets the right difficulty. Right or wrong, you'll learn something on every answer.

↓ every answer

The reveal is the point, not the score

Answer and the explanation opens immediately, whether you were right or wrong. It says which option was correct, why the tempting wrong one is wrong, and then names the source it was written from. The example below cites Davis and Goadrich's 2006 paper on precision-recall versus ROC curves.

That citation is doing real work. A multiple-choice question with a debatable key is worse than no question, so every item is written against a public reference, checked by an independent review pass hunting for ambiguous keys and giveaway option lengths, then read by a human before it counts.

There are 645 reviewed questions behind the topics that are live today, mapped onto 230 concepts.

↓ the result

A map, not a grade

The score is one line. Underneath it sits the knowledge map: every concept the topic covers, coloured by how you did on the questions that touched it, with the prerequisite links drawn in. Concepts your six questions never reached stay dashed and empty rather than being guessed at.

Tap any node for what the concept is and how to close it, plus a direct route back into a fresh set of questions on it. Above the map, a rule-based diagnosis says in a sentence what the run actually showed, including when the honest read is that six questions weren't enough to be sure.

A clean 6/6. You handled ROC-AUC vs PR Curve — the hardest idea in this set — without slipping. Take it again to cover confusion matrix; a perfect run on fresh questions is the real test.

↓ calibration

Boring math, on purpose

Item difficulty starts as an estimated prior and then recalibrates from real responses. Session ability updates the same way, with a K that decays as an item collects answers, so an established question stops swinging on any one person's result.

R = 1500 − 400·log₁₀(p / (1−p))  ·  K_item = 32 / (1 + n/40)  ·  K_session = 48

Ability starts at 1350, 1500 or 1650 depending on which way the placement question routed you. Only first exposures feed calibration, so retaking a topic can't inflate the item statistics. Every rule here is a few lines of explainable code, and the whole thing is on the methodology page rather than in a footnote.

↓ the number it won't fake

A percentile needs 30 sessions before it says anything

"Top 27% of 412 this month" means exactly that: your result placed against every real session on that topic in a rolling 90-day window. Until a topic clears 30 sessions, there is no such comparison to make, so the result says early data and stops. Your first attempt is the one that locks the percentile; retakes draw unseen questions but don't let you shop for a better rank.

The same rule governs the community dashboard. It is built and wired, and it currently reports the one thing it can honestly report, which is how many checks you have contributed from this device.

This page will show checks taken this week, the most-attempted topics, live question recalibration events, and the monthly job-market pulse — once the beta has real traffic to report. No fake numbers in the meantime.

↓ your résumé

Which of these you already have on paper

A career track is a named role with its six topics named too. Open one cold and it tells you the truth immediately: nothing assessed, and here are the six things this job wants evidence of.

Paste your résumé, or photograph it. SkillCompass matches it against that track's own curriculum and marks which subtopics your experience already evidences, pulling out the concrete skills it found rather than a vague seniority label.

Each evidenced topic then gets a Verify button, which is the honest part. A résumé claim is a claim. Verifying it means taking the two-minute check and finding out whether the thing you wrote down survives six questions.

The text is used for a single call and never stored, whether pasted or uploaded. Nothing about it reaches a database, and the result stays on your device. Where a job-postings corpus would let this compare you against live market demand, there isn't one yet, so the report compares against the curriculum and says so on the page instead of inventing salary bands.

This gap report compares you against this track's own curriculum. A live job-market panel (top skills in this month's postings, salary context) arrives in a later beta phase.

↓ the capstone

An interview that asks a second question

Assess all six topics in a track and the mock interview unlocks. It runs six to eight turns of real text conversation, pitched at the seniority your results actually demonstrated rather than the one you claimed, and it follows up when an answer is thin instead of accepting it. It ends with a per-dimension scorecard.

It is stateless by design. The transcript is resent with each turn, so there is no server-side session holding your conversation, and nothing to leak. Testing it against a deliberately vague answer got that answer flagged and scored low across several follow-ups, which was the point of the test.

The third feature is the study guide: a downloadable Markdown plan for a whole track, built from your real per-concept results and, where they exist, your résumé and interview too. All three run on one shared model call helper, with the turn-taking and validation written in plain Python.

↓ your data

Two buttons: export, and erase

Everything you do lives in one localStorage key on your own browser. The Progress page lists it back to you, every check with its date and score, and gives you a button to take it away as a file and a button to delete it outright. Neither asks you to log in first, because there is no login.

The only thing recorded centrally is an anonymous answer log: question id, right or wrong, response time. That is what powers calibration. It never records who you are, and there is no account for it to attach to.

↓ the rest of it

Twelve smaller things

Keyboard the whole way

Press A–D to answer and Return to advance. A check takes about two minutes without your hand leaving the keyboard.

Skip the placement

Already know your level? Start easier or start harder and go straight to the set you want.

Unseen-first retakes

Take a topic again and it draws questions you haven't met. Your first attempt still owns the percentile.

Dark mode

A full night theme on its own toggle, with the palette validated for contrast in both directions.

Photograph your résumé

No file to hand? Upload a photo or screenshot and it gets read the same way, in memory, once.

Feedback endpoint

The API takes structured feedback already. Putting a report link on every question is the next step, and the methodology page says so rather than implying it's done.

Pace, not a clock

Nothing counts down. Your seconds-per-question is shown afterward as information, never as a penalty.

Partial tracks are labelled

A track with four of six banks ready says so and shows a readiness badge, rather than looking complete and failing halfway.

Certificate prep

CFA Level 1, Google Data Analytics and AWS Cloud Practitioner are mapped in the catalog, marked coming soon until their banks land.

Resume where you left off

The home page picks your track back up at the topic count you'd reached, from the same local store.

Daily AI capacity, in public

The free model tier has a ceiling. The methodology page shows today's call count against it and says plainly what happens at the top.

Deep links work

A result has its own URL you can come back to, served through a 404 redirect shim so a static host handles app routes.

Arrive · what it's built on

Free, and free to run

SkillCompass costs $0 a month to operate. That is a design constraint rather than a boast: nothing here depends on a budget that could be cut, and no part of it needs to be monetised later.

Frontend
React, Vite and TypeScript, served as a static build from GitHub Pages. The entire quiz loop runs here
API
Python FastAPI on a Hugging Face Space, free CPU tier. Percentiles and the three AI features only
Calibration store
Supabase Postgres, free tier, reached through a stdlib-urllib PostgREST client rather than an SDK
AI features
Gemini 2.5 Flash on the free tier, called over plain REST, with thinking tokens switched off so the visible output isn't truncated
Content pipeline
A generator drafts questions in difficulty bands, an independent reviewer pass repairs or rejects them, and a human signs off before anything counts
Question sources
Named public references, cited on the question itself. Curricula referenced against Coursera, edX and Kaplan
Your data
One localStorage key. An anonymous answer log is the only central record
